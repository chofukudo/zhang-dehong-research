1. Why an update is needed: three new facts

A research note that needs updating within 48 hours usually has one of two causes: the market produced new material, or the data produced a new reading. This time both happened at once.

No.213's sample window ended at the close of October 22, 2026. That session compressed into a striking set of numbers: the Nikkei fell 2.65%, KOSPI 3.52%, the Hang Seng 3.03%, the SOX 6.72% and the Nasdaq 2.60%, while the Dow closed flat and more than half of S&P 500 constituents rose. The source material defined the day as "an echo of the AI-hardware retreat that began around June 22" and dissected four faces: Japan fell on the arithmetic of price weighting, Korea on the double concentration of exports and market caps, Taiwan's earnings were not sold, and China was an internal rotation rather than an external decoupling. Four months of dispersion were pre-written by four variables — index construction, export concentration, the earnings event window and the funding structure. That was No.213's core judgment.

This report answers one question: after the ebb day, does that judgment still stand?The answer requires three sets of new material.

The first new material is the global close of October 23.The market did not continue in one direction: the Shanghai Composite closed at 3,690.48, up 0.89%; the Shenzhen Component rose 2.79%, the ChiNext 4.16% and the STAR 50 3.04% — A-shares completed a V-shaped reversal overnight, with more than 4,500 stocks rising. KOSPI, down more than 4% in early trading, turned higher in the afternoon on Nvidia-related news, closing at 5,550.30, up 0.30%; SK Hynix gained 4.41%. The SOX closed at 10,779.46, up 0.44%, touching an intraday high of 10,923.18. The Nikkei was the only major Asian index still lower, closing at 62,693.16, down 0.34%. In US trading, Nvidia and Apple traded the "most valuable company" title back and forth intraday; Nvidia finally closed at $210.96 (-0.59%), a market cap of $5.08trn. The most informative stock of the day was CoreWeave: it fell another 2.46%, to $54.235, taking its market cap below the $30bn round number.

These numbers tell a story that differs from October 22 yet is highly isomorphic to it: the deepest losses did not repair immediately, the shallowest rebounded most, and the weakest link of the funding chain kept sinking.Isomorphism deserves suspicion: if October 23 were a "bounce day", why does the rebound ranking match the loss ranking so closely? The answer points to a common factor: the market is re-pricing semiconductor exposure and funding intensity, and that process did not end within 24 hours.

The second new material is Meta's Q3 2026 results.The report, released after the close on October 21, fell after the source material was drafted but outside its verification window — the source flagged "-$2.12bn in Q3 free cash flow" as "pending verification" because "it could not be found in public filings". In fact the report had been published: Q3 revenue of $64.601bn, up 26% year on year; operating cash flow of $32.79bn; capex (including finance-lease principal payments) of $34.91bn; free cash flow of -$2.12bn — the first quarter of negative free cash flow since Meta's IPO.The company simultaneously raised the lower bound of its 2026 capex guidance from $130bn to $135bn (upper bound unchanged at $145bn) and announced publicly traded non-voting C-class shares, opening a new financing channel. In one quarter, Meta completed the transition from self-financing to needing to knock on the capital-markets door. The source's caution was appropriate; this update can now settle it: this is not a typo, not an accounting convention, but a watershed in the funding structure.

The third new material is a re-estimate of the depth of China's internal rotation.The source concentrated on the Shanghai Composite (-12.1% from June 22 to October 22) and the CSI 300 ETF (drawdown 19.3%). This report recomputes the same window for the ChiNext and STAR 50: ChiNext fell 40.89% from its late-June high region, STAR 50 33.00%, while the Shanghai Composite fell only 12.13% — a gap of nearly 30 percentage points."China assets are being sold" fails even at the aggregate level: what fell was not Chinese assets but the most expensive segment of Chinese assets.The distinction was confirmed in reverse on October 23 — ChiNext rebounded 4.16% while the Shanghai Composite rose 0.89%; money rotated the direction of the trade back within a single day.

Together, the three new facts point to a conclusion steeper than the source's: the breadth of the ebb (how many assets are falling) is beginning to narrow; its depth (how far the funding structure has deteriorated) is not. In the language of ships: the water surface is already rising, but the vessel's waterline is still descending. The task of this update is to re-measure every notch of that waterline.

An update matters not because it adds one more day of data, but because it adds one more class of evidence. October 23 supplies counter-evidence, Meta's results supply confirmation, and the A-share rotation supplies deepening. All three point the same way: the ebb is not over; it has merely changed face.

Methodologically, this report holds to one discipline: event research does not change its verdict on the basis of a single candle, and the verification ledger is not relaxed because a narrative is smooth.Once the October 23 rebound appeared, the "ebb over" reading became instantly popular; this report's judgment rests on statistics (isomorphic ranking, volatility, CDS), not on candlestick shape. The same discipline applies to the source's "pending" items: Meta's turn negative was upgraded to "verified" because of the company filing, not because of the market's reaction to it. Writing the two together is meant to show that this update shares the same evidence-grading standard as the source — new data may upgrade a conclusion, but the upgrade must go through the ledger, not through emotion.

Around June 22, 2026, the year's highs clustered: KOSPI peaked at 9,114.50 on June 22, the SOX around 14,634.7 in mid/late June, and the CSI 300 ETF at 5.091 on June 22. Over the following four months, the AI-hardware complex suffered its steepest drawdown of the cycle — but the four markets fell in four different ways.

The mechanisms are developed chapter by chapter below; a skeletal recap suffices here. Japan's fall was "arithmetic": the Nikkei 225 is price-weighted, so high-priced semiconductor-equipment names (Tokyo Electron, Advantest, Screen) carry disproportionate weight; the semiconductor sell-off was amplified by index construction — on October 22 the Nikkei fell 2.65% while the TOPIX (via 1305.T) fell only 1.23%, a ratio of 2.15 versus a median of 1.12 since 2025. Korea's fall was "concentrated": September exports of $120.94bn (+83.5% y/y) and semiconductor exports of $60.33bn (+262.8% y/y) set records in the very quarter in which KOSPI lost 39.3% from its peak, because the conditional correlation between Samsung and SK Hynix reached 0.934 and the index's moves are set almost single-handedly by the memory cycle. Taiwan fell least: TAIEX lost only 3.6% from June 22 to October 22, TSMC outperformed the market in the six sessions after earnings, and the CAR[0,+5] of +1.47% was statistically indistinguishable from zero — "sell-the-news" was falsified. China's fall was "rotation": the Shanghai Composite fell 12.1% while ChiNext fell 40.9% and bank stocks made new highs — what fell was the most expensive segment, and what held was the segment with the most certain cash flows.

Four months of dispersion matter because they rule out single-factor explanations. If this were an "AI bubble burst", the S&P 500 should not have risen 2.6% over the same period; if it were an interest-rate shock, all long-duration assets should have fallen together; if it were risk-off, the Dow should not have closed flat. The only reading compatible with the four-month numbers is: the market is re-pricing a specific exposure — semiconductors and AI infrastructure — while moving money into assets not exposed to it.That is rotation, not a crash.

That judgment sets the yardstick for October 23: if rotation is ongoing, the next day should show "the deepest do not repair, the shallowest keep rising"; if rotation is over, we should see a broad repair or a broad confirmation. October 23 delivered the former — which is precisely what this report sets out to demonstrate.

The four faces are not four markets but four variables: index construction, export concentration, the earnings event window and the funding structure. October 22 exposed them all at once; October 23 tested whether they still operate.

3. Japan's skew: arithmetic acts before sentiment

On October 22 the Nikkei 225 closed at 62,907.84, down 1,713.40 points (-2.65%), the second-steepest loss among major Asian indices. But half of that number is arithmetic, not sentiment.

The Nikkei is price-weighted: the higher a constituent's share price, the greater its influence on the index, regardless of market capitalisation. Tokyo Electron, Advantest and Screen trade at tens of thousands of yen and carry far more weight in the Nikkei than in the TOPIX's market-capitalisation scheme. When semiconductor selling appears, their losses are mechanically amplified. On October 22 the Nikkei fell 2.65% while the TOPIX (1305.T proxy) fell 1.23% — two indices measuring the same market, answering twice as differently.

The source measures this skew with the absolute difference between Nikkei and TOPIX daily returns (NT_abs); this report recomputes on the same definition. NT_abs averaged 0.430 in 2025 (n=227) and rose to 0.736 in 2026 (n=184); a rank-sum test gives z=-4.731 (p<0.001) and a Kolmogorov-Smirnov D of 0.2345 — the skew shifted decisively higher in 2026. On October 22, NT_abs was 1.446, the 84.8th percentile of 2026: elevated, but not extreme.

That 84.8th percentile deserves a second reading. The original source claimed a Z-score of +2.63 for the Nikkei-TOPIX return gap that day, using it to characterise the day as an extreme index-construction anomaly. Recomputing on the full 2025-01-to-2026-09 distribution (mean 0.564, standard deviation 0.545) gives a Z of only 1.62. The difference between 2.63 and 1.62 is not a decimal point; it is the difference between "extreme event" and "elevated but not extreme". The truer picture is subtler: 29 sessions in 2026 had a skew of at least 1.446. The number looks extreme only because 2025's base was low.

No.213 conducted an honest self-check: the ratio of Nikkei to TOPIX one-day losses (2.15) was about twice an ordinary day's, but the median of that ratio since 2025 has drifted between 1.12 and 1.22, without a monotonic trend. The absolute gap (NT_abs) is rising while the relative ratio is not, implying that part of the widening comes from higher overall volatility and part from shifts in weight structure — the two sources cannot be fully separated in this dataset. That limitation carries into this update unchanged: it does not affect the direction of the conclusion, only its precision.

October 23 adds a telling detail: the Nikkei closed at 62,693.16, down 0.34% — the only major Asian index still lower, while KOSPI turned up 0.30%, Taiwan and A-shares rebounded sharply, and the SOX closed green. Why was Japan alone down? Two sources: the Nikkei's semiconductor weight (Advantest, Tokyo Electron and peers) did not receive the same after-hours tailwind from US chips; and the Nikkei's own skew persists — as long as high-priced semiconductor names drag the index, its reading will be gloomier than the TOPIX's. If the TOPIX outperformed the Nikkei again on October 23, NT_abs stayed elevated into the next day, and the skew has not subsided.

The Nikkei's problem is never Japan's problem; it is arithmetic's problem. Judging a market-capitalisation-weighted market with a price-weighted index is like using a magnifying glass as a ruler: the reading is always larger than the truth, and the larger it is, the more distorted.

4. Korea's concentration: export records do not save an index

Korea is the most extreme case of this ebb and, in data terms, the cleanest. KOSPI peaked at 9,114.50 on June 22 and closed at 5,533.77 on October 22, down 39.3%; over the same span SK Hynix fell from 2,919,000 to 1,223,000 won (-58.1%) and Samsung Electronics 40.8%. Four months, four-tenths, two stocks — the Korean index is held hostage by a single point of exposure: the memory cycle.

It is one of the great ironies of this cycle that Korean exports set records in the same period. Ministry of Trade data show September exports of $120.94bn, up 83.5% year on year; semiconductor exports of $60.33bn, up 262.8%; exports to the US up 137% and to China up 123%. Yonhap reported on October 14 that September ICT exports exceeded $70bn for the first time, a record. The coexistence of an export boom and a stock-market crash in the same month is itself a mockery of "fundamentals drive prices": the fundamentals did not deteriorate; what deteriorated was the valuation's exposure to a single variable.

The October 15 collapse provides the most vivid illustration of concentration. KOSPI fell 7.57% to 5,554.22 that day, Samsung fell 9.48% and SK Hynix 11.06%, and both KOSPI and KOSDAQ triggered circuit breakers. The trigger was the plunge in CoreWeave's bonds (explicitly cited by Xinhua as the day's trigger for Asia) compounded by a marginal shift in Fed expectations. Using a GARCH(1,1) two-step estimator, the source computed standardised residuals for Samsung and SK Hynix: on October 15, -2.77 and -2.47, both beyond the ±2 extreme threshold; on October 22, -0.93 and -1.07, both inside it. October 15 was Korea's own event day; October 22 was not.KOSPI's 3.52% loss on October 22 was a following move on the global semiconductor factor, not an independent Korean shock.

The other face of concentration is correlation. The unconditional correlation between Samsung and SK Hynix is 0.807; over the last 20 sessions it rose to 0.908, and on October 22 the conditional correlation was 0.934 (Z=1.13). The source corrected the original claim of "a jump from 0.64 to 0.81 on the event day" — the level is higher (0.934), but the "event-day jump" does not hold: 0.934 is not unusual in the distribution, and high correlation is the norm, not a new fact of October 22. The correction matters: the pair's portfolio risk is structural, not event-driven. Two positions in these stocks are not two positions; they are one position, doubled.

October 23 delivered the most dramatic sequence of this update: KOSPI fell more than 4% in early trading, almost replicating October 22, then reversed violently in the afternoon on Nvidia-related news (statements by Jensen Huang), closing at 5,550.30, up 0.30%; SK Hynix closed up 4.41%. An intraday swing of more than 4.5 percentage points shows Korea's pricing in a tug-of-war between "memory-cycle expectations" and "the global semiconductor factor" — Hynix's rebound leading KOSPI suggests that memory carries the greatest elasticity to a stabilising SOX, and the least stability.

Read together, October 15, 22 and 23 trace a clean evolution: event day (10/15) → following day (10/22) → rebound day (10/23).Each move was triggered by an external signal (bonds, the SOX, Nvidia), while domestic export data never entered the pricing equation. The portfolio implication is direct: for the foreseeable future, KOSPI remains a high-beta proxy for global semiconductors, and export fundamentals offer almost no buffer at the index level.

Worth separate attention is the composition of Korea's export record — it is the mirror image of concentration risk. Semiconductor exports of $60.33bn (+262.8% y/y) were driven overwhelmingly by memory (DRAM and HBM); exports to the US and China both surged more than 120%, showing that Korea's export boom depends on synchronised global AI-capex expansion. When the driver of the record (AI servers, HBM, advanced packaging) and the driver of share prices (memory-cycle expectations) come from the same source, export data lose their hedging function: they are not evidence of diversification but the other side of concentration. On October 23, SK Hynix's 4.41% gain versus KOSPI's 0.30% again confirmed that the market prices Hynix as "SOX beta plus a memory option", not as a footnote to exports. To track Korea, watch the SOX's overnight close, not the monthly export release.

Korea's lesson in one sentence: record exports and a 40% index decline can both be true. Concentration filters out the good news from fundamentals and amplifies only the bad news from the cycle.

5. Taiwan's saturated expectations: a falsified narrative

Taiwan is the quietest of the four faces and, methodologically, the most important. Its value lies not in TSMC but in providing a testable counter-example: when the market's favourite story is "perfect fundamentals plus a falling share price", an event study is what dismantles it.

TSMC reported Q3 2026 on October 15: revenue of NT$1,494.24bn, up 50.9% year on year; net income of NT$763.06bn, up 68.7%; diluted EPS of NT$29.43; gross margin of 66.4%; advanced-node revenue share of 79%; Q4 guidance of $52.3-54.3bn. Every line beat, and the company disclosed that 2nm revenue had already reached 8% of the mix, 3nm 33%, 5nm 28% and 7nm 10%. The report's strength gave "sell-the-news" traders their material: TSMC's stock wobbled in the following sessions, and some claimed "the good news was priced in and sold".

The source tested this claim with an event study: event day October 15, estimation window [-121,-2], market model benchmark TAIEX, beta 0.934 (t=19.07), R²=0.773; the event-window CAR[0,+5] was +1.47%, t=0.63, statistically indistinguishable from zero; among 500 random six-day placebo windows, the true CAR sat at the 68.6th percentile — the centre-right of the neutral zone. The correct reading of the three numbers together: there is no evidence of "sell-the-news", and no evidence that the market welcomed the report either.Statistically, TSMC's post-earnings performance was indistinguishable from random; economically, it outperformed the index (which weakened over the same window).

The original source's CAR of -2.75% (t=-2.41) is opposite in sign and opposite in significance. Such discrepancies usually come from three places: the wrong event day, overlap between estimation and event windows, or the wrong benchmark. The source specifies all three (event day 10/15, window [-121,-2], benchmark TAIEX), so it is reproducible — writing a positive CAR as negative and dressing it up as "sell-the-news" is narrative driving conclusions, not data driving them.

Taiwan's market structure also provides a buffer: TSMC's earnings visibility, advanced-node pricing power and diversified customer base all exceed Korea's memory duopoly. From June 22 to October 22, TAIEX fell only 3.6% while KOSPI fell 39.3%; on October 22 Taiwan fell 1.81%, the lightest among major Asian markets. With equally heavy semiconductor weight, the thickness of the buffer determines the size of the fall.

October 23 continued the pattern. TSMC's ADR opened at $412.29 (previous close $412.35), touched an intraday high of $421.31 and closed in the upper part of the range, up more than 2% intraday at the peak, while the SOX closed green. Eight sessions after the report, the ADR sits above its pre-earnings close; on any event-window definition, this earnings report cannot be read as a sell signal.

The most memorable thing about Taiwan is not how good TSMC is, but how important method is: when a share price falls for reasons unrelated to earnings, insight cannot see it — an event window can. Turning a positive CAR into a negative one requires not data but narrative courage.

6. China and Hong Kong: rotation, not decoupling

The source material contained an elegant claim: China has statistically decoupled from the global AI cycle, with the 60-day rolling correlation between CSI 300 and the SOX near zero. No.213 falsified it: "the claim was true at a point in time, and wrong in October." Recomputing with daily returns of the Shanghai Composite and the SOX, the 60-day rolling correlation in 2026 ran: -0.004 at end-February, 0.016 end-March, 0.118 end-April, 0.213 end-May, 0.349 end-June, 0.407 at end-July and end-August, 0.397 end-September, 0.316 on October 22. The true decoupling window was February-March 2026; thereafter the two re-coupled month by month.Moving February's reading to October is using a six-month-old conclusion as today's.

This update deepens the recomputation of China's internal rotation with three new sets of numbers, all supporting and strengthening the source's judgment.

Set one: the rotation is deeper than it looked.Recomputing the same window, June 22 to October 22: Shanghai Composite -12.13%, CSI 300 ETF (510300) drawdown -19.33%, ChiNext -40.89%, STAR 50 -33.00%. The gap between the outer numbers is nearly 30 percentage points.What fell was not Chinese assets but the most expensive segment of Chinese assets.This is not rhetoric — if "China is being sold" were true, the Shanghai Composite and ChiNext should fall together; instead the loss was entirely concentrated in the high-valuation growth segment, closely aligned with exposure to the AI-hardware chain.

Set two: October 22's fall was not extreme within 2026.Across the 192 trading sessions of 2026 to date, the Shanghai Composite's -1.48% ranked at the 7.8th percentile — deeper than only 7.8% of days, hardly an extreme event; ChiNext's -4.37% ranked at the 3.1st percentile, near-extreme. The contrast is itself the statistical portrait of rotation: calm at the index level, turmoil inside the growth segment. Turnover on October 22 was CNY 1.53trn; ICBC rose 1.66% to an all-time high, the motor sector rose 4.13%, while the optical-module chain was crushed (Innolight -12.28%, Zhongji Innolight -8.76%, TFC Optical -8.36%). Money left AI hardware for banks and high-dividend names — a transfer of valuation, not a loss of capital.

Set three: October 23 proves the rotation runs in both directions.This is new to this report. On October 23 the Shanghai Composite rose 0.89% to 3,690.48, the Shenzhen Component 2.79%, ChiNext surged 4.16%, STAR 50 3.04%, the CSI 300 ETF 1.73%, with more than 4,500 stocks rising. But ICBC fell 3.14% and Agricultural Bank of China 2.84% — the high-dividend leaders that made new highs on October 22 gave it all back — while Innolight, Zhongji Innolight and TFC Optical rebounded 1.16%, 3.82% and 1.93%.Money rotated the direction of the trade back within a single day: from banks to optical modules.Two conclusions follow. First, "Chinese assets" do not have a unified price. Second, A-share rotation operates at a daily frequency, and any one-way "abandon AI, take dividends" call will be slapped by the market the next day.

Hong Kong's triple exposure is unchanged. The Hang Seng fell 3.03% to 22,934.68 on October 22, HSCEI 3.20%, and Hang Seng Tech fell more than 5% intraday to a two-year low; Alibaba fell 6.32%, Meituan 4.66%, Xiaomi 3.80%, Tencent 2.94%, while China Mobile fell only 0.26%. Hong Kong hangs on three ropes: China's fundamentals, global liquidity and local events. October 23 saw Hong Kong remain weak (platform stocks did not V-reverse as A-shares did), a sharp contrast with the mainland's strong rebound — another demonstration of the three ropes: A-shares' discount rate is set largely at home, while half of Hong Kong's is set abroad.

China's October also carries a frequently missed asset-price signal: the divergence in long-end rates. On October 22 the 10-year Treasury yield fell 19.3bp (5.146% to 4.953%) while the 10-year CGB did not move in tandem, and the China-US spread actually widened on the event day. The source corrected the original claim of "long-end yields spiking" (the direction was wrong; October 22 was a decline). This update keeps that correction and adds a mechanism: the asynchrony of Sino-US rates is precisely why A-shares' February "statistical decoupling" was possible. A-shares' discount rate is set by the domestic credit cycle; the US yield affects Hong Kong through global risk appetite and affects foreign-held A-share core assets through liquidity expectations, but it never prices the whole A-share market directly. On October 22, A-shares fell 1.48% and ChiNext 4.37% while banks made new highs — a domestic re-allocation under lower-rate expectations, almost unrelated to the US curve's direction that day. To classify Chinese assets simply as "global risk assets" is to systematically overstate the transmission of the Fed path into Chinese valuations, and to misjudge direction when the next February-style decoupling window opens.

China's October is not decoupling; it is rotation. Not loss of blood; a change of address. Put bank new highs, optical-module limit-downs, a 40% ChiNext decline and a full reversal the next day into one week, and the 1.48% Shanghai decline reads as a rotation receipt, not a sell notice.

7. The US: beneficiaries and financiers priced apart

The October 22 US dispersion: SOX -6.72%, Nasdaq -2.60%, S&P 500 -1.21%, Dow +0.02%. October 23: SOX +0.44%, Nasdaq recouping intraday, the Dow weak (down more than 1% intraday), and Nvidia and Apple trading the "most valuable company" title. Two days combined tell one story: the market has not rejected AI; it has begun to distinguish "those who sell the shovels" from "those who borrow to buy shovels".

The micro-foundation of that distinction is cash flow. Split the AI value chain into two classes. Beneficiaries sell chips, equipment and foundry capacity; their revenue grows with capex and they do not need to finance it. Financiers build data centres, buy compute and pay electricity bills; capex is a cost, not revenue. When the cost of financing rises, the first class sees its valuation affected by the discount rate; the second, by the discount rate plus solvency. The difference in the two elasticities is the source of the dispersion.

October 22's cross-section read that distinction directly: CoreWeave -10.06% (the deepest in the sample), Meta -6.98%, AMD -8.45%, Micron -6.46%; Microsoft -2.23%, Oracle -2.82%, Alphabet -3.20%. The ranking matches "funding intensity" far better than "AI exposure" — AMD's AI exposure is no lower than Microsoft's, but it fell more because its valuation carries more cyclical leverage; CoreWeave fell hardest because its business model is, literally, borrowing to buy compute and renting it out.

The funding-side cracks gained three new pieces of evidence in this update; this report verifies each.

First, and most important: Meta's free cash flow turned negative for the first time since its IPO.Q3 2026 (released October 21): operating cash flow $32.79bn, capex (including finance-lease principal) $34.91bn, free cash flow -$2.12bn. For reference, Q3 2025 free cash flow was +$10.625bn; nine-month cumulative FCF collapsed from $29.50bn a year earlier. Meta simultaneously raised the lower bound of full-year capex guidance ($130bn to $135bn) and created non-voting C-class shares — burning cash while opening the financing door. On October 22, Meta fell 6.98%, erasing about $124bn of market value.The source flagged "-$2.12bn" as pending verification; this update confirms it: it is a fact, and it is a watershed.In three quarters, Meta went from the thickest cushion of the four (Q2 2026 FCF +$1.7bn, coverage ratio 0.35) to the first company among the majors to need to hold out its hand since listing.

Second: CoreWeave's credit pricing is still deteriorating.As of October 21, its five-year CDS spread stood at 1,085bp, up 14% week on week and 164% over the year; unsecured bonds yield about 15%, and the 2032 notes trade below 80 cents on the dollar. On October 22 the stock fell 10.06%; on October 23 it fell another 2.46% to $54.235, market cap below $30bn — two-thirds below the roughly $90bn peak three months after listing. UBS (per an October 24 report) cut its target price to $105 and warned that "restoring investor confidence could take several quarters". Debt stands at about $51.6bn; the order book of about $129bn still faces a financing gap of roughly $20bn.CoreWeave is the marginal issuer of the whole funding chain: whether the bond market opens its door is first visible in its CDS.

Third: the four-cloud cash-flow ledger is updated beyond Q2 2026.The source recomputed quarterly capex and FCF for the four majors from Q2 2025 to Q2 2026, with the core findings: Microsoft never went negative (Q2 2026 FCF +$19.64bn), Alphabet turned negative in Q2 2026 (-$5.85bn), Amazon was negative in Q1 2025 and Q1 2026 (-$18.17bn), and Meta's cushion ran out. This update adds Meta's official Q3 numbers, completing the spectrum: Microsoft is self-financing, Alphabet is tightening, Amazon is volatile, Meta has just slid into the externally-financed class, and CoreWeave is in survival mode.A blanket statement that "hyperscaler cash flows are deteriorating" yields a conclusion that is right in direction and wrong in magnitude; and a wrong magnitude becomes a wrong weight in a portfolio.

Alphabet's side of the story supports the "tightening" label. Public reports say Alphabet raised about $80bn of debt in October to fund AI data-centre expansion (with Berkshire Hathaway among the main backers), on top of its negative Q2 2026 FCF (-$5.85bn). The search giant's capital structure is shifting from cash-funded investment to debt-financed expansion. Similarly, Nvidia has been reported to be building a roughly $500bn financing platform to fund large AI projects — the upstream chip seller using debt instruments to finance downstream customers.When every link of the chain starts to rely on financing rather than cash flow, the marginal cost of funding stops affecting only one class of company and starts affecting the valuation anchor of the whole chain.Alongside Meta's C-class shares and CoreWeave's CDS, these events complete the Q4 2026 picture: it is not one company short of cash; the entire AI-capex cycle is switching from profit-financed to debt-financed.

The counter-evidence must also be written down: on October 23 more than half of S&P 500 constituents rose and the SOX closed green, so the market is not discounting the whole complex on "funding deterioration"; Nvidia briefly fell 0.19% before retaking the top spot, showing that "AI leader" pricing survives, just without the reflexive uptrend. This report keeps the source's formulation and adds a layer: the funding structure is the depth of this ebb, not its trigger; it explains the distribution of losses, not their occurrence.CoreWeave's lone decline on October 23 is the depth's first solo apparition after the ebb.

Four financial statements read together say not "AI does not make money" but "capex growth is outrunning operating-cash-flow growth". This is a timing mismatch, not a profitability problem. Mismatches can be repaired by time or prolonged by financing; Meta's Q3 chose the latter.

11.4 Hidden assumptions, at least five

First, "rotation is the best description of China" assumes that bank-stock strength is cash-flow-driven, not speculative crowding; if banks are themselves a crowded trade, the "rotation" reading is a rotation into another bubble.
